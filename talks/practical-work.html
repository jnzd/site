Motivation

Why formalize the law?

Laws are written in natural language, but much of what they say has a logical structure: conditions, obligations, exceptions, deadlines. Writing that structure down formally has three benefits.

  • It shows the logical structure. A formalization removes some ambiguity and lets us reason clearly about what a provision means.
  • It opens the law to a broader audience. A formal reading can make parts of a law easier to understand, and automated tools built on it can make the law more accessible.
  • It enables automation. With runtime verification, we can check that the behaviour of a computer system complies with the law.

Formalizing the law for runtime verification

Runtime verification comes in two forms. Monitoring checks, online or offline, that a system's behaviour complies with a policy. Enforcement goes further: it runs alongside the system and can correct its behaviour, so it is inherently online.

State-of-the-art enforcement tools require the law to be encoded as logical formulas. Translating legal text directly into formulas is difficult and error-prone, and the result is hard to check against the original. A good formalization should therefore also be readable.

This work

Lex is an intermediate language between the law in natural language and runtime verification policies written as logical formulas. It stays readable by keeping the hierarchical structure of the law, and it has mechanisms that make the logical structure explicit.

Formalizing the law: some theory

Vagueness and the human element

Vagueness is inherent to the law, and scholars debate whether it could ever be eliminated entirely. Lex does not try to. It formalizes the logical structure of legal texts and leaves vague terms as inputs: where a provision is ambiguous, a human decides, and that decision is given to the system as an event.

The logical structure of the law

  • Exceptions. Legal texts often specify when another section of the law does not apply.
  • Brute and institutional facts. Brute facts are “facts of nature”, such as a person's age or a car's speed. Institutional facts are derived from brute facts: a person may vote in Switzerland if they are over 18 and a Swiss citizen, so “having the right to vote” is an institutional fact.

This structure differs from that of common programming languages, which is why Lex has dedicated constructs for it.

Three kinds of rules

A toy example shows the three kinds of rules Lex distinguishes:

  1. Obligation rule: To enter the building, authorization is required.
  2. Constitutive rule: Employees are authorized to enter the building.
  3. Exception rule: During time off, (2) does not apply.

Obligations say what must hold, constitutive rules derive institutional facts such as “authorized”, and exceptions switch other rules off. The thesis formalizes this as .

Temporal logic

Metric first-order temporal logic

MFOTL extends first-order predicate logic ($\phi \land \psi$, $\phi \lor \psi$, $\neg\phi$, $P(x)$, …) with temporal operators. Each operator carries an interval $I$ that bounds how far into the future or past it looks.

Always$\Always_I \phi$Historically$\Historically_I \phi$
Until$\phi \UUntil_I \psi$Since$\phi \Since_I \psi$
Next$\Next_I \phi$Previous$\Prev_I \phi$
Eventually$\Eventually_I \phi$Once$\Once_I \phi$

Future operators are on the left, past operators on the right. The thesis defines the logic in .

Enforceable MFOTL

Enforceable MFOTL is the policy language of the WhyEnf enforcer , a subset of MFOTL in which every predicate is marked with a capability:

  • Causable: the system can create the event, e.g. “send a notification to the user”.
  • Suppressable: the system can prevent the action, e.g. “a user makes a public comment”.
  • Observable: the system can take no action on the event.

A type system decides whether a formula can be enforced: a formula typed $\Cau$ can be made true by causing events, one typed $\Sup$ can be made false by suppressing them. Two of its rules:

$$\prfrule{$\neg^{\Cau}$}{\Gamma \vdash \phi : \Sup}{\Gamma \vdash \neg\phi : \Cau} \qquad \prfrule{$\lor^{\Cau}_{L}$}{\Gamma \vdash \phi : \Cau}{\Gamma \vdash \phi \lor \psi : \Cau}$$

The Lex language

Goals for Lex

  • High readability, to be evaluated in a planned user study.
  • Close correspondence to legal texts in structure and form.
  • Relatively easy auditing of whether a formalization accurately represents the law it formalizes.
  • Two compilation targets: a documentation-like HTML representation of the formalization, and a logical formula that recent runtime enforcers can enforce. The compiler checks the enforceability requirements itself.

A Lex file mirrors the hierarchy of the law it formalizes. Here is 26 U.S. Code § 121, “Exclusion of gain from sale of principal residence”. The US Code nests titles, chapters and sections more deeply than most laws, so Lex lets a level repeat: title[1] is a level below title.

Example: data processing under the GDPR

Obligation rules

Article 5(1)(a) of the GDPR requires personal data to be processed lawfully, fairly and transparently. In Lex, this is an obligation rule. The generated documentation shows the rule next to its plain-English reading and the original text.

article 5 law GDPR / chapter II
XML: GDPR Grundsätze für die Verarbeitung personenbezogener Daten
paragraph 1 law GDPR / chapter II / article 5
point a law GDPR / chapter II / article 5 / paragraph 1
        rule
          whenever
            PersonalDataProcessing(ep, x, z)
          oblige
            lawfulness(ep)
            fairness(ep)
            transparency(ep)
        enforceable
        suppressing PersonalDataProcessing
Reading

Whenever all of the following happen:

  • x is processing personal data z as part of processing operation ep

Then the following shall be obligatory:

  • processing ep is lawful
  • processing ep is fair
  • processing ep is transparent

XML: GDPR Personenbezogene Daten müssen […] a) auf rechtmäßige Weise, nach Treu und Glauben und in einer für die betroffene Person nachvollziehbaren Weise verarbeitet werden („Rechtmäßigkeit, Verarbeitung nach Treu und Glauben, Transparenz“);

The rule is marked enforceable: the compiler must be able to enforce it by suppressing PersonalDataProcessing.

Constitutive rules

When is processing lawful? Article 6(1)(a) answers: “Processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies: (a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes” (GDPR Art. 6). A constitutive rule derives the institutional fact lawfulness(ep) from brute facts.

article 6 law GDPR / chapter II
XML: GDPR Rechtmäßigkeit der Verarbeitung
paragraph 1 · point 1 · subpoint a law GDPR / chapter II / article 6
        rule
          whenever
            PersonalDataProcessing(ep, x, z)
            HasPurpose(ep, prp)
            ONCE (GiveConsent(w, c) AND Authorizes(c, prp))
            ONCE Nominates(y, x)
            PersonalData(z, w)
          constitute
            lawfulness(ep)
Reading

Whenever all of the following happen:

  • x is processing personal data z as part of processing operation ep
  • processing operation ep has purpose prp
  • at some point in the past, data subject w gave consent c, and consent c authorizes usage of personal data for purpose prp
  • at some point in the past, controller y nominated processor x to process personal data on y's behalf
  • z is personal data of data subject w

Then the following shall be constituted:

  • processing ep is lawful

Exception rules

Article 8 sets conditions for a child's consent. In a simplified form: consent given by a minor does not make processing lawful. The exception refers to the rule it disables by its section label.

article 8 law GDPR / chapter II
XML: GDPR Bedingungen für die Einwilligung eines Kindes in Bezug auf Dienste der Informationsgesellschaft
paragraph 1 law GDPR / chapter II / article 8
        rule
          whenever
            isMinor(w)
          except
            article 6 paragraph 1 point 1 subpoint a
Reading

Whenever all of the following happen:

  • data subject w is a minor

Then article 6 paragraph 1 point 1 subpoint a shall not apply.

Lifting the EMFOTL type system to Lex

An obligation rule $r : \phi \Rightarrow \psi$ is complied with (made true) in one of three ways: suppress its conditions, cause its effects, or cause one of the exceptions to the rule.

$$\prfrule{REG$_L$}{r : \phi \Rightarrow \psi \in R \quad \Gamma \vdash \phi : \Sup}{\Gamma \vdash \comply(r) : \Cau} \qquad \prfrule{REG$_R$}{r : \phi \Rightarrow \psi \in R \quad \Gamma \vdash \psi : \Cau}{\Gamma \vdash \comply(r) : \Cau} \qquad \prfrule{REG$_E$}{\Gamma \vdash \exc(r) : \Cau}{\Gamma \vdash \comply(r) : \Cau}$$

The GDPR obligation above uses the first way: it is enforced by suppressing PersonalDataProcessing.

Temporal patterns

Conditions in Lex can carry a temporal pattern written in words. Each pattern compiles to an MFOTL operator and determines whether the condition looks into the past or the future, which in turn affects how it can be typed.

The compilation pipeline

The compiler passes a Lex file through four intermediate representations before writing the files WhyEnf reads.

  1. .lex file
  2. Parse Lex code
  3. lex
  4. Type terms, compute labels, collect exceptions
  5. tlex
  6. Sort rules topologically, check enforceability
  7. elex
  8. Prepare signature and MFOTL formula
  9. clex
  10. Compile and linearize as strings
  11. WhyEnf files
